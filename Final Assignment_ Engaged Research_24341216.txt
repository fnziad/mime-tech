Introduction:

My early understanding of mime was shaped by Western popular culture—specifically the antics of Mr. Bean. That illusion fractured during a live performance at BRAC University, where silence functioned not as comedy but as confrontation. The performers’ gestures carved meaning into the air, communicating social grief and resistance without speech. I searched for the history of mime in Bangladesh, expecting a documented lineage, but encountered only fragmented social media clips and flyers without archives. The art form was not extinct—it was unrecorded. This dissonance between lived intensity and documented silence became the impetus for my research into Bangladeshi mime and its precarious existence beyond institutional recognition, where an Epistemic Void threatens its historical survival.

Since the anti-colonial movements through the July 2024 Uprising, performance has served as a vital medium of social commentary in Bangladesh, particularly when speech is censored or surveilled. Mime has repeatedly functioned as historical witness, yet it remains structurally marginalized. While theatre benefits from institutional patronage and curricular presence, mime occupies a condition of institutional invisibility—excluded from national archives, state platforms, and academic syllabi. This vulnerability has become more urgent amid recent volatile socio-political landscape of December 2025, where attacks on cultural institutions have revealed the fragility of physical records and informal memory. This contradiction raises a central inquiry: how can a performative form so integral to Bangladesh’s history of resistance survive without formal documentation, and what kinds of digital infrastructures are necessary to safeguard its transmission, legitimacy, and continuity?


Bangladeshi mime currently faces three interrelated challenges. First, a documentation crisis: performances disappear without professional recording, limiting artists' access to international funding and exposing significant work to erasure. Second, economic precarity: mime practitioners operate informally, receive inconsistent compensation, and lack institutional mechanisms for representation. Third, a knowledge vacuum: embodied techniques are transmitted orally or textually, with no audiovisual pedagogical system to sustain intergenerational training or public legitimacy. Addressing these challenges within an eight-month timeline and a 500,000 BDT budget, this project proposes the Mime Tech-Collective (MTC), a community-led digital intervention developed in collaboration with troupes such as Dhaka University Mime Action (DUMA) and Shawon Mime Academy maintaining ethical protocols, fair compensation, and collaborative governance. This paper argues that the sustainability of Bangladeshi mime depends on bridging its ephemeral practice with permanent infrastructure. It does so through three strategic interventions: (1) an active documentation unit that records live performances to produce a curated, citable digital archive; (2) a professional representation platform that assists artists with portfolios and negotiation, restoring artistic legitimacy through standardized management tools; and (3) an Academic Legitimization Pipeline that preserves embodied practices and generates institutional recognition—thereby preserving the past, sustaining the present, and securing the future of Bangladeshi mime.




Rationale:
Bangladeshi mime is at a critical juncture, with its practitioners standing as undocumented bearers of a profound cultural legacy. Embodying a lineage of silent artistic resistance that has long served as a vital form of social commentary in Bangladesh (Ahmed, 2023), their contributions remain unseen, trapped in the ephemerality of live performance and absent from the national record. This study is therefore necessary to counter this institutional neglect. By creating a permanent digital archive, the project validates these artists not as peripheral entertainers but as an invaluable part of the art community whose silent language articulates the nation's struggles and aspirations.


The urgency of this project is amplified by the socio-political volatility of December 2025, where arson attacks on revered cultural institutions like Chhayanaut have proven that physical infrastructure is no longer a secure repository for heritage. Mime artists, who served as non-violent historical witnesses during the July 2024 Uprising, are custodians of the nation's ethos and pathos. Their silent, constructive form of protest offers a powerful alternative to the destructive 'noise' of mob violence, a sentiment echoed by DUMA founder Mir Lokman's lament from abroad: "Is this truly the language of protest? Burning and killing?" Unless their ephemeral work is documented now, this unique physical language of dissent risks being erased by both political revisionism and infrastructural fragility. As a Computer Science student positioned to mediate between performance and technology, my objective is to build a digital sanctuary—an accessible, permanent archive that secures the legacy of these artists against both the slow erosion of time and the immediate threat of physical erasure, ensuring their historical significance is preserved.
Literature Review
The precarious existence of Bangladeshi mime is not an isolated phenomenon but rather a symptom of broader tensions within postcolonial performance culture. To understand why an art form so vital to the nation’s political history remains institutionally homeless, one must navigate the theoretical intersections of performance ontology, political history, and the sociology of culture. A critical review of existing scholarship—ranging from performance theory to local historiography—reveals a significant gap: while the vulnerability of mime is well-theorized, the infrastructural mechanisms required for its survival in a digital age remain unexplored. This review synthesizes these disparate threads to argue that the proposed Mime Tech-Collective (MTC) is not merely a technical project, but a necessary intervention to bridge the gap between ephemeral performance and permanent institutional legitimacy.
The Ontology of Erasure: The Archive and the Repertoire
The central theoretical dilemma facing Bangladeshi mime is its resistance to documentation. Unlike literature or painting, mime is an embodied art that vanishes in the moment of its creation. Performance studies scholar Diana Taylor (2003) provides the foundational vocabulary for this crisis by distinguishing between the "archive" of enduring materials (texts, buildings) and the "repertoire" of embodied practice (dance, ritual, mime). Taylor argues that Western epistemology privileges the archive, often dismissing the repertoire as lacking historical weight. She notes:
Archival memory works across distance, over time and space... The repertoire, on the other hand, enacts embodied memory: performances, gestures, orality, movement, dance, singing—in short, all those acts usually thought of as ephemeral, nonreproducible knowledge. (Taylor, 2003, p. 20)
This distinction is critical for understanding the "Epistemic Void" identified in this project. Bangladeshi mime exists almost entirely within the "repertoire"—in the muscle memory of practitioners like Shariar Shawon and the oral traditions of troupes like Dhaka University Mime Action (DUMA). Because it has not been transferred to the "archive," it remains invisible to the state and academia.
Peggy Phelan (1993) complicates this further by arguing that this disappearance is the very definition of performance. In her seminal work Unmarked, she asserts:
Performance’s only life is in the present. Performance cannot be saved, recorded, documented, or otherwise participate in the circulation of representations of representations: once it does so, it becomes something other than performance. To the degree that performance attempts to enter the economy of reproduction it betrays and lessens the promise of its own ontology. (Phelan, 1993, p. 146)
While Phelan’s argument highlights the unique power of the live event, this project posits that in the context of a developing nation like Bangladesh, such "ontological purity" is a luxury that marginalized artists cannot afford. As the recent history of the July 2024 Uprising demonstrates, performances that are not "saved" or "documented" are susceptible to historical erasure. Therefore, this project argues against Phelan’s resistance to documentation, proposing instead that a Digital Living Archive is the only way to protect the "repertoire" from being forgotten in a volatile political landscape.
The Local Context: Performance in the "Culture of Fear"
The necessity of archiving is underscored by the socio-political history of performance in Bangladesh. Shafi Ahmed (2023) traces the lineage of Bangladeshi theatre from traditional folk forms like Yatra to anti-colonial resistance and the socialist influence of the IPTA, establishing a legacy where the artist serves as a political witness. This tradition is epitomized by the slogan "natak hok gono-manuser hatiar" ("Let the theatre be people’s means of protest"), which gained prominence as a tool of activism during the military regimes of the 1980s (Chakroborthy, 2023, p. 115). However, this role has become increasingly precarious; Sudip Chakroborthy (2023) introduces the concept of Bhoyer Songskriti or the 'Culture of Fear,' noting that in the twenty-first century, 'self-censorship is the norm' due to the threat of sectarian violence and political coercion (p. 120).
It is within this Bhoyer Songskriti that mime emerges as a potent, yet vulnerable, tool. Because it relies on silence and gesture rather than text, mime can bypass the explicit censorship that restricts spoken theatre. This was evident in the production Rakte Agun Legechhe ("The Blood is on Fire"), staged by Mime Art to commemorate the July 2024 Uprising; the performance depicted "mass killing" and the "role of journalists" who were forced to hide the truth, communicating these realities without a single word (New Age, 2025). Yet, as Banerjee (2013) argues, for such community-based theatre to have a lasting social impact, it must be "mixed with long-term developmental planning" (p. 15). Without a permanent infrastructure, these acts of resistance remain fleeting. The arson attacks on physical institutions in Dhaka, December 2025 validate the need for the Mime Tech-Collective’s decentralized repository, as digital agency becomes the only viable sanctuary for a history that physical spaces can no longer secure.
The "Gold Book" and the Battle for Indigenous Identity
A recurring struggle for Bangladeshi mime is the stigma of being a "Western import," often dismissed as mere imitation of Charlie Chaplin or Marcel Marceau. This misconception is rigorously deconstructed by the only comprehensive primary text on the subject: Echoes of Silence: Exploring the Timeless Art of Mime by Dr. Fr. Tapan De Rozario (2025). This book serves as the critical "missing link" in the literature. De Rozario explicitly argues that Bangladeshi mime is not a derivative art but a syncretic one, rooted deeply in indigenous traditions. He writes:
Traditional Bengali performance forms like jatra, pala gaan, and alkap... rely heavily on exaggerated gestures, facial expressions, and choreographed movement- all of which resonate with mime. Modern Bangladeshi mime artists often draw upon these traditions to craft pieces that reflect local folklore, societal values, and historical memory. (De Rozario, 2025, p. 134)
This insight is revolutionary for the project’s rationale. It proves that the "grammar" of Bangladeshi mime (what this project aims to digitize) is a unique cultural heritage, distinct from European traditions. However, De Rozario also admits the "epistemic void" that this project seeks to fill. In his preface, he laments that mime "remains under-theorized in scholarly literature and often marginalized within mainstream theatrical discourse" (De Rozario, 2025, p. 77). By identifying this gap, De Rozario provides the academic mandate for the Mime Tech-Collective’s Academic Legitimization Pipeline—a system designed to translate his static text into dynamic, citable digital assets.


The Economy of Neglect: Institutional Cultural Capital
While the artistic value of mime is clear, its economic viability is precarious. Priyanka Basu (2019) offers a critical lens through her concept of "institutionalized cultural capital." She argues that folk and marginal performance genres often struggle to survive because they lack the "state sanction" that confers legitimacy and funding (p. 245). In Bangladesh, this is manifested in the exclusion of mime from state-sponsored talent hunts like Notun Kuri.
The media narrative often masks this precarity. A recent article in The Financial Express titled "The Art of Silence: How Bangladesh is Embracing the Mime" paints a rosy picture, claiming that "Mime has a growing presence in Bangladesh, with active troupes and a vibrant community" (Fateha, 2025). However, this journalistic optimism contradicts the structural reality identified by Basu. While the community is vibrant, the infrastructure is hollow. The article mentions the "Bangladesh Mime Federation" and "Shawon Mime Academy," yet fails to note that these entities often lack physical offices or permanent funding. This disconnect between the 'official narrative' of growth and the 'ground reality' of professional informality justifies the project’s Professional Representation Platform. Since the state fails to provide physical infrastructure, the "Agency Model" must provide digital infrastructure to generate the "institutional capital" Basu describes.
Digital Solutions for Marginalized Arts
If the problem is structural and economic, the solution must be technological. Rocky Prasetyo Jati (2019) provides a relevant precedent from Indonesia, demonstrating how karawitan communities utilized low-cost digital media to preserve their heritage. Jati observes that by using platforms like YouTube and Facebook, these communities establish an "alternative space" for communication that functions as a form of "resistance to the mainstream media" (p. 491). This "bottom-up" approach, which allows marginalized groups to bypass traditional institutional barriers, directly inspires the Mime Tech-Collective’s (MTC) strategy
However, simply uploading videos is insufficient. The UNESCO (2017) Guidance Note for Inventorying Intangible Cultural Heritage emphasizes that all interactions with practitioners must be contingent upon their "free, prior and informed consent" (Ethical Principle 4). The document highlights that while inventorying involves both top-down and bottom-up processes, communities must play the "primary role" in safeguarding their own history. This ethical framework is the guiding principle for the MTC; unlike commercial platforms, the MTC is designed as a community-led support system where artists retain ownership of their data. This aligns with a "Service-Learning" model, where technology serves the artist as a tool for legitimacy, rather than the artist serving the platform.
Conclusion: Bridging the Gap
The literature reveals a clear trajectory. Taylor and Phelan establish the theoretical vulnerability of ephemeral art. Ahmed and Chakroborthy locate this vulnerability within the specific political volatility of Bangladesh. Father Tapon De Rozario provides the historical and pedagogical foundation, proving that the art form is indigenous and robust. Finally, Basu and Jati offer the economic and technological frameworks for understanding how marginalized arts can survive.
However, a critical gap remains. There is no existing literature that proposes a concrete, operational model for digitizing the specific "grammar" of Bangladeshi mime or for creating a "Digital Agency" to solve the economic precarity of its practitioners. The existing "Gold Book" is a static resource in a kinetic world; the existing "Federations" are political bodies without economic engines. This paper addresses this void. By proposing the Mime Tech-Collective, it synthesizes the theoretical need for an archive with the practical need for an economy, ensuring that the "Language of Silence" is not lost to the noise of history.


Community Map and Interview Analysis
To understand the lived reality of the mime community, I conducted a "stratigraphic" analysis, interviewing practitioners representing the diverse layers of the ecosystem. The research began at BRAC University with the "New Generation" represented by Fahim Akhand Plabon (Secretary, BUDTF) and Kazi Anonna Rahman Preeti. Through their referrals, the study moved to the "Site of Resistance" at TSC to interview Mohammad Shahjalal Shoron (General Secretary, DUMA), and finally to the veteran master Shariar Shawon. This fieldwork was spatially grounded in a Community Map (Figure 1) that revealed a dramatic fragmentation: while student activists organize at the political core of the city, internationally recognized masters are forced to operate in informal spaces due to a lack of institutional infrastructure.

The first layer of analysis focused on the students, revealing a profound "Knowledge Vacuum." Plabon, a microbiology student with nine performances to his name, articulated a sense of homelessness within the discipline: "There is no central place that I can go and learn mime... There is no one who is doing that because none of it is actually recognized properly" (F. A. Plabon, personal communication, December 7, 2025). This statement validates the epistemic void; without a centralized academy, young artists face an identity crisis. Preeti added that general audiences often "lose their connection" because they view the art form merely as comedy rather than a sophisticated language. This data indicates that the crisis is not just about funding, but about legitimacy; students are hesitant to pursue a career in an art form that offers no clear pedagogical path.
[Figure 2: Photo of student performances/rehearsals. Caption: Figure 2. Student practitioners like Plabon operate within university clubs, isolated from the broader professional lineage of the art form.]
The second layer involved Master Shariar Shawon. The disconnect between his global acclaim and local reality was jarring. Our interview took place in a tea stall in Hazaribagh, a physical manifestation of the "Institutional Homelessness" facing the community. Shawon emphasized the "Recognition-Funding Trap," explicitly lamenting the state's exclusion of mime from national platforms: "If you look at 'Notun Kuri'... there is poetry, song, dance... but there is no mime. This is where mime has been excluded in many ways. There is no patronage" (S. Shawon, personal communication, December 9, 2025). This circular failure traps top-tier professionals in an informal economy, stripping them of the bargaining power their work merits.
[Figure 3: Photo of Shariar Shawon performing. Caption: Figure 3. Master artist Shariar Shawon. Despite international tours, the exclusion from state platforms like Notun Kuri forces professional negotiations into informal spaces.]
The final layer was provided by Shoron, representing the political lineage of DUMA. He identified a critical "Documentation Crisis." He observed that while mime was hyper-visible as a tool of protest during the July 2024 Uprising, this history is being systemically deleted because there is no authoritative archive. Shoron articulated this fear of erasure in a moving reflection:
I personally believe that it needs to be written somewhere in history. Because most of the history can be changed with a big fat lie and a lot of blood. For which we believe that if it comes down from the generation to generation through art... the generation will understand what it is. Rather than the one the historians write—no offense to them—but it can be altered, right? (M. S. S. Shoron, personal communication, December 9, 2025)
[ Figure 4: Screenshot of a DUMA performance at TSC or the "Gold Book" cover. Caption: Figure 4. The fragility of history. Shoron argues that without a secure archive, the narrative of resistance is vulnerable to political revisionism.]
This fieldwork precipitated a decisive pivot in the project design. Initially, I hypothesized that the solution was "Visibility"—simply creating a social media page. However, the data dismantled that assumption. Plabon does not need a Facebook page; he needs a curriculum. Shawon does not need "likes"; he needs a booking system that demands professional respect. Shoron does not need a viral video; he needs a secure archive that prevents the "big fat lie" of revisionism. This "grounded theory" approach forced a shift from a passive awareness campaign to the active Mime Tech-Collective (MTC)—a student-led service agency designed to provide the specific infrastructural pillars that the community currently lacks.


Step-by-Step Plan of Action
To address the systemic triad of institutional invisibility, documentation gaps, and epistemic voids, this project proposes the establishment of the Mime Tech-Collective (MTC). The MTC is not merely a website, but a University-incubated Service Agency. It functions as a centralized digital and operational hub that provides active infrastructural support to the fragmented mime community. By establishing this infrastructure, the project aims to bridge the stark gap between Bangladesh’s informal sector and the institutionalized mime ecosystems seen in countries like France, where archives and academies (e.g., L'École Jacques Lecoq) protect the art form.

Pillar 1: Active Documentation & Archival Service
The foundation of the agency is the Mobile Documentation Unit. A team of paid student interns from the University’s Media/Film Department will be deployed as a rapid-response crew. They will attend performances by partner troupes (DUMA, Mime Art) to capture high-definition footage using mime-specific cinematography.
* The Backend: While the front-end remains a professional MTC Archive, the backend utilizes cost-effective platforms like YouTube (for streaming/publicity) and Zenodo (for DOI/academic citation), creating a dual-layer archive that is both accessible and scientifically rigorous. For long-term archiving, Zenodo (a CERN-backed open repository)  automatically assigns Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) to every upload, making the footage citable. Furthermore, all uploads will be tagged using Dublin Core metadata standards (Creator, Title, Date, Subject: "July Uprising") to ensure interoperability with global library systems.
* The Output: Artists receive professional showreels to build their portfolios, while the MTC secures the historical record against erasure.
Pillar 2: The Digital Professionalization Platform (The "Agency" Core)
To solve the economic precarity of informal negotiations, the MTC establishes a Centralized Web Platform acting as a "Virtual Impresario."
* The Student Manager Program: Recognizing that the local event industry operates on informal networks (phone/WhatsApp) rather than cold web forms, the platform is supported by Student Managers. These interns act as intermediaries for the artists—handling inquiries, negotiating rates based on a standardized scale, and generating professional contracts. This human buffer protects artists from logistical harassment and low-balling, allowing them to focus entirely on their craft.
* Sustainability Model: Initially, these services are subsidized by the pilot budget. In the long term, the MTC will transition to a Contribution Model, where established artists or commercial clients pay a minimal service fee (e.g., 5%) to sustain the platform, while services remain free for student groups.
* The Living Academy (LMS): The website will house a scalable Learning Management System. While the pilot phase operationalizes Chapter VI of Father Tapon’s Echoes of Silence with Master Shariar Shawon, the architecture is designed to expand. Future modules will invite other prominent artists (e.g., Mir Lokman, Nithor Mahbub) to record their unique techniques, creating a comprehensive, pluralistic library of Bangladeshi mime that rivals international digital curricula.
Pillar 3: The Academic & Institutional Pipeline
To solve the "Epistemic Void," the MTC will function under a formal Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) with the University’s Theatre & Performance Studies Department.
* Research Integration: The MTC will provide the department with exclusive access to the digitized archives and metadata. In exchange, the department will integrate mime research into its curriculum, assigning final-year students to write monographs on the archived works.
* Ethical Licensing: To balance "open access" with "artist protection," all archived materials will be licensed under Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). This legal framework ensures that while researchers and students can freely view and cite the work (solving invisibility), no commercial entity can exploit the footage without a separate paid contract (protecting economic dignity).
* State Collaboration: The agency will leverage its organized data (viewership metrics, historical archives) to lobby State Cultural Departments and BTV. By presenting professional "Press Kits" and data reports, the MTC creates the "Institutional Weight" necessary to demand inclusion in national programs like Notun Kuri.

How would the project serve the community?
The Mime Tech-Collective (MTC) serves the community by shifting the power dynamic from "survival" to "sustainability." Primarily, it restores economic dignity to practitioners who have been structurally undervalued. By intervening in the negotiation process through the "Student Manager" program, the MTC provides artists with the administrative weight of an institution. This ensures that masters like Shariar Shawon are no longer treated as informal street performers but as contracted professionals with standardized rates and technical requirements. The service creates a buffer that allows artists to focus on their craft rather than the humiliation of bargaining, effectively generating the "institutional cultural capital" necessary to secure fair wages and state grants.
Beyond economics, the project provides "Citational Justice." Currently, Bangladeshi mime is invisible to history because it generates no data. By professionally recording performances and assigning them Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs), the MTC transforms ephemeral acts into permanent, citable evidence. This serves the broader cultural community by ensuring that the history of resistance—specifically the artistic contributions to the July 2024 Uprising—is preserved in a secure, community-owned archive rather than being lost to political revisionism. It forces future historians, researchers, and policymakers to acknowledge the art form not as a footnote, but as a primary source of national heritage.
Finally, the project serves the next generation by democratizing access to knowledge. For students who currently face an "identity crisis" due to the lack of a central academy, the Living Academy provides a structured, accessible pathway to mastery. By digitizing indigenous techniques and linking them to Bengali traditions like Jatra, the project dismantles the stigma of mime as a "Western import." It validates the students' artistic choice, providing them with the pedagogical roots and professional tools needed to carry the lineage forward into the digital age.


Effectiveness of the Project
The projected effectiveness of the Mime Tech-Collective (MTC) relies on its departure from the "Passive Archive" model—where databases often become digital graveyards because artists lack the time or technical literacy to maintain them. Instead, the MTC adopts an "Active Intermediary" model, similar to successful gig-economy platforms in Bangladesh (e.g., Sheba.xyz or Pathao), which provide a managed digital layer over an informal service sector. By deploying Student Managers to handle the "digital friction" of uploading, tagging, and negotiating, the MTC ensures high adoption rates. The artists do not need to change their behavior; the infrastructure wraps around them. This "human-in-the-loop" design mitigates the risk of "technological rejection" often seen in development projects where complex tools are forced upon non-technical communities.
Furthermore, the project’s sustainability is theoretically grounded in the pedagogy of Service-Learning. As defined by Bringle and Hatcher (1996), successful university-community partnerships require a "reciprocal exchange" of value. The MTC is not a charity; it is a credit-bearing laboratory. The Theatre and Media students are incentivized by academic credit and portfolio building, creating a renewable, cost-effective labor force that does not depend on perpetual grant funding. This structural integration with the university ensures that the documentation machinery continues to function even if external donor interest wanes.
Finally, the project ensures effectiveness through standardization. In the music industry, the "Technical Rider" is the standard mechanism that enforces professional respect. By introducing this legalistic tool to the mime community, the MTC creates a "normative pressure" on organizers. When a venue receives a professional PDF rider from an agency, the perceived cost of non-compliance increases. This shift from informal verbal agreements to standardized digital contracts is the specific mechanism that will break the "hierarchy of neglect," proving that digital tools can generate tangible offline dignity.


Conclusion
The survival of Bangladeshi mime relies on resolving the critical disconnect between its immense cultural value and its profound institutional neglect. This research has argued that the Mime Tech-Collective (MTC) serves as the necessary digital intervention to bridge this gap, transforming a fragile, ephemeral practice into a resilient, professionalized discipline through active documentation, economic mediation, and academic archiving. By shifting the focus from passive observation to active infrastructural support, the MTC addresses the core crises of "institutional homelessness" and "epistemic silence" that currently threaten the art form.
This study began by diagnosing the precarious reality of practitioners like Shariar Shawon, who possess international acclaim yet lack basic professional infrastructure, and the historical vulnerability identified by Shoron, where the narrative of resistance risks erasure. The proposed solution responds directly to these findings. Through the Mobile Documentation Unit, the project secures the "language of resistance" against political revisionism; through the Student Manager Program, it restores economic dignity by enforcing professional standards; and through the Academic Legitimization Pipeline, it converts the "indigenous grammar" of mime into citable scholarship. Ultimately, while physical institutions remain vulnerable to the volatile political landscape, this digital infrastructure offers a permanent sanctuary. It ensures that the silent labor of these artists is finally accorded the institutional weight it deserves, preserving a vital history for the future. We may not be able to build a brick-and-mortar academy overnight, but we can build a digital home that ensures the silence of these artists continues to speak louder than the noise of history.
